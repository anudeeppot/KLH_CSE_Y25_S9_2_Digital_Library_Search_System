Content: CHAPTER 1: THE DEMOCRATIC CITATION MODEL OF SCIENTIFIC VALUE
In early digital library search systems, document ranking was governed strictly by lexical query matching (such as TF-IDF). However, in vast corpora containing millions of academic dissertations and conference papers, thousands of documents often match a user's search query with similar keyword frequencies.
In 1998, Stanford doctoral students Larry Page and Sergey Brin formulated PageRank, establishing that the structural topology of hyperlinks (and academic citations) provides an objective, democratic measure of document importance.
In the context of digital libraries, a citation from paper A to paper B represents an academic endorsement: paper A confers a portion of its scholarly prestige to paper B. However, an endorsement from a prestigious, highly-cited monograph (such as Knuth's The Art of Computer Programming) carries vastly more authority than a citation from an obscure, unverified student essay.

CHAPTER 2: THE RANDOM SURFER AND MARKOV CHAIN FORMULATION
Definition 2.1 (The Random Surfer Model):
Consider a virtual researcher browsing through an academic digital library.
1. With probability alpha (the damping factor, typically 0.85), the researcher follows an outgoing citation link chosen uniformly at random from the current paper's bibliography.
2. With probability 1 - alpha, the researcher becomes bored and teleports (jumps) to a completely random document chosen uniformly from the entire library collection.
This process forms an ergodic Markov Chain over the discrete state space of library documents V = {1, 2, ..., N}.

Definition 2.2 (The Transition Probability Matrix M):
Let A be the adjacency matrix of the citation graph, and let d_{out}(i) be the out-degree (number of outgoing citations) of document i.
The normalized stochastic transition matrix P is defined by:
P_{i, j} = 1 / d_{out}(i)    if document i cites document j, else 0.
If document i is a dangling node (no outgoing citations, d_{out}(i) = 0), its row is replaced with uniform transitions: P_{i, j} = 1 / N for all j.
To guarantee that the Markov chain is irreducible and aperiodic, the Google Matrix G is defined as:
G = alpha * P + ( (1 - alpha) / N ) * E
where E is the N x N all-ones matrix.

CHAPTER 3: THE PERRON-FROBENIUS THEOREM AND POWER ITERATION
Theorem 3.1 (Perron-Frobenius Theorem):
Because all entries of the Google Matrix G are strictly positive (G_{i, j} >= (1 - alpha) / N > 0), G is a primitive, irreducible, and regular stochastic matrix.
By the Perron-Frobenius Theorem:
1. The largest eigenvalue of G is lambda_1 = 1.
2. The algebraic and geometric multiplicity of lambda_1 is 1.
3. The corresponding principal left eigenvector pi is strictly positive (pi_i > 0 for all i) and unique when normalized such that sum_{i=1}^N pi_i = 1.
4. For any initial probability distribution r_0, the iterative power sequence r_{k+1} = r_k * G converges exponentially to the stationary distribution pi:
lim_{k -> infty} r_k = pi.
The rate of convergence is governed by the second largest eigenvalue lambda_2, where |lambda_2| <= alpha = 0.85. The error decreases by a factor of 0.85 at each iteration, requiring only 30 to 50 iterations to achieve 6 decimal digits of precision across a million documents.

CHAPTER 4: POWER ITERATION ALGORITHM SPECIFICATION
Algorithm 1: PageRank-Power-Iteration(G, alpha, epsilon)
Input: Graph G = (V, E), damping factor alpha (0.85), convergence threshold epsilon (1e-6).
Output: Stationary distribution vector r of length N.
1. Initialize r_0 = [1/N, 1/N, ..., 1/N]^T.
2. Repeat:
     a. Compute dangling node mass: S = sum_{i: d_{out}(i)=0} r_{old}[i].
     b. For each node j in V:
          r_{new}[j] = (1 - alpha) / N + alpha * ( S / N + sum_{(i, j) in E} ( r_{old}[i] / d_{out}(i) ) )
     c. diff = sum_{j=1}^N |r_{new}[j] - r_{old}[j]|.
     d. r_{old} = r_{new}.
   Until diff < epsilon.
3. Return r_{new}.
