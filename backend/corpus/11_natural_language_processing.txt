Content: CHAPTER 1: THE TEXT NORMALIZATION PIPELINE IN DIGITAL LIBRARIES
Before any document in a digital library can be indexed or searched by algorithmic engines (such as KMP, Suffix Arrays, or inverted indices), raw character streams must undergo rigorous text normalization.
Academic documents exhibit complex linguistic idiosyncrasies: hyphenated line breaks, typographical ligatures (such as 'fi' and 'fl'), non-ASCII mathematical symbols, diacritics, and domain-specific punctuation (e.g. 'Ph.D.', 'et al.', and equations).
The standardized normalization pipeline consists of four sequential stages:
1. Sentence Segmentation: Breaking running text into discrete sentence units using rule-based boundary detection (evaluating punctuation context and honorific abbreviations).
2. Tokenization: Splitting sentences into atomic linguistic units (tokens).
3. Case Folding and Diacritic Stripping: Mapping characters to canonical Unicode forms (e.g. Unicode Normalization Form C - NFC).
4. Stemming and Lemmatization: Reducing inflectional morphological variants (e.g. 'retrieving', 'retrieved', 'retrieval') to their common lemma base form ('retrieve').

CHAPTER 2: SUBWORD TOKENIZATION AND BYTE-PAIR ENCODING (BPE)
Traditional word-level tokenization suffers from the Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) problem: when a user searches for an obscure compound term, medical neologism, or technical acronym not present in the pre-compiled dictionary, word-level systems fail completely. Conversely, pure character-level tokenization produces excessively long sequence representations that degrade search latency.
The modern standard for digital text indexing is subword tokenization via Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE).
Originally developed by Philip Gage in 1994 as a data compression technique, BPE was adapted for natural language processing by Sennrich et al. in 2016.
Algorithm 1: Byte-Pair Encoding Training
Input: Raw training text corpus, desired vocabulary size V.
1. Initialize vocabulary V with all individual unique characters (and byte symbols) in the corpus.
2. Represent every word in the training corpus as a sequence of space-separated characters plus an end-of-word symbol '</w>'.
3. Repeat until |V| reaches the target threshold:
     a. Count the frequency of all adjacent symbol pairs across the entire corpus.
     b. Identify the most frequent symbol pair (A, B).
     c. Create a new vocabulary token AB and add it to V.
     d. Replace all co-occurrences of 'A B' with 'AB' across the corpus.
4. Output the learned merge rules and subword vocabulary.
By preserving frequent words as whole tokens while decomposing rare technical words into meaningful subword constituents (e.g. 'string' + 'ology'), BPE provides optimal vocabulary compactness for digital library indexing.

CHAPTER 3: N-GRAM STATISTICAL LANGUAGE MODELING
Definition 3.1 (The N-Gram Probability Distribution):
An N-gram language model estimates the probability of the next word w_k given the preceding history of N - 1 words w_{k-N+1} .. w_{k-1}.
Under the Markov assumption of order N - 1:
P(w_1, w_2, ..., w_n) = prod_{k=1}^n P(w_k | w_{k-1}, w_{k-2}, ..., w_{k-N+1})
The Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE) computes this conditional probability via relative frequency counts:
P_MLE(w_k | w_{k-1}, ..., w_{k-N+1}) = Count(w_{k-N+1} .. w_k) / Count(w_{k-N+1} .. w_{k-1})

Definition 3.2 (Smoothing and Backoff: Kneser-Ney):
In realistic library corpora, many valid word combinations have Count = 0 in the training data, leading to catastrophic zero probabilities.
Interpolated Kneser-Ney smoothing resolves this by combining absolute discounting of higher-order counts with lower-order continuation probabilities:
P_KN(w_i | w_{i-1}) = ( max(Count(w_{i-1}, w_i) - d, 0) / Count(w_{i-1}) ) + lambda(w_{i-1}) * P_continuation(w_i)
where P_continuation(w_i) measures the fertility of word w_i—how likely it is to complete novel histories across the academic library.

CHAPTER 4: PART-OF-SPEECH (POS) TAGGING AND SYNTACTIC DISAMBIGUATION
When search engines process queries containing polysemous terms (such as 'book' as a noun vs 'book' as a verb, or 'mining' in data mining vs mineral extraction), syntactic disambiguation is required.
Using Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), the optimal sequence of grammatical tags T = (t_1, t_2, ..., t_n) for word sequence W = (w_1, w_2, ..., w_n) is decoded via the Viterbi dynamic programming algorithm:
T* = argmax_T prod_{i=1}^n P(w_i | t_i) * P(t_i | t_{i-1})
This enables facet filtering in the digital library, allowing users to restrict queries to noun phrases, method titles, or authorial attributions.
