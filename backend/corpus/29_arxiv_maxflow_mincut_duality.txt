Content: CHAPTER 1: DUALITY AND FLOW IN COMBINATORIAL OPTIMIZATION
In discrete mathematics and algorithmic graph theory, duality is the profound principle that every maximization problem possesses an intrinsic, mathematically equivalent minimization problem.
In 1956, Lester R. Ford and Delbert R. Fulkerson proved the celebrated Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem, establishing that the maximum data or commodity throughput achievable across a capacitated directed network is strictly equal to the capacity of the network's most constrained bottleneck cut.
In academic digital libraries, this duality governs two critical operational systems:
1. Campus CDN Bandwidth Optimization: Distributing high-resolution digital manuscripts and research datasets across bandwidth-constrained campus gateways.
2. Optimal Resource Allocation and Student Reservation Matching: Assigning limited physical copies, reserve textbooks, and digital access licenses to student reservations to maximize fair academic access.

CHAPTER 2: THE EDMONDS-KARP ALGORITHM AND POLYNOMIAL GUARANTEES
The original Ford-Fulkerson method augments flow along arbitrary paths in the residual network G_f. If edge capacities are irrational, Ford-Fulkerson may fail to terminate or converge to an incorrect suboptimal flow value. If capacities are large integers, it may require O(E * |f|) augmentations.
In 1972, Jack Edmonds and Richard M. Karp proved that choosing the shortest augmenting path (in terms of edge count) via Breadth-First Search (BFS) guarantees strongly polynomial termination in strictly O(V * E^2) time.

Lemma 2.1 (Monotonicity of Shortest Path Distances):
Let d_f(s, v) be the shortest path distance (number of edges) from source s to vertex v in the residual network G_f.
As flow is augmented along shortest paths, for all vertices v in V - {s, t}, the distance d_f(s, v) increases monotonically:
d_{f'}(s, v) >= d_f(s, v).
Proof:
Suppose for contradiction that there exists a vertex v whose distance decreases after an augmentation. Let v be the vertex with the minimum new distance d_{f'}(s, v) among all such vertices.
Let (u, v) be the last edge on the shortest path from s to v in G_{f'}:
d_{f'}(s, v) = d_{f'}(s, u) + 1.
By our choice of v, vertex u cannot have decreased its distance: d_{f'}(s, u) >= d_f(s, u).
If (u, v) was already an edge in G_f:
d_f(s, v) <= d_f(s, u) + 1 <= d_{f'}(s, u) + 1 = d_{f'}(s, v), contradicting the assumption that distance decreased.
If (u, v) was not in G_f, it must have been added by augmenting flow along the reverse edge (v, u) in G_f:
Therefore, d_f(s, u) = d_f(s, v) + 1.
Then d_{f'}(s, v) = d_{f'}(s, u) + 1 >= d_f(s, u) + 1 = d_f(s, v) + 2, a clear contradiction!
Thus, shortest path distances never decrease.

Theorem 2.1 (Edmonds-Karp Bound):
An edge (u, v) becomes critical (saturates its capacity) at most |V| / 2 times during the entire algorithm execution. Since there are |E| edges, the total number of augmenting path iterations is at most O(V * E). Each BFS takes O(E) time, yielding total time O(V * E^2).

CHAPTER 3: DINIC'S BLOCKING FLOW ACCELERATION
In 1970, E. A. Dinic introduced layered graphs, computing all augmenting paths of a given length simultaneously as a 'blocking flow' in O(V * E) time per phase.
Because the shortest path distance from s to t increases strictly by at least 1 at each phase, there are at most |V| - 1 phases.
The total time complexity of Dinic's algorithm is strictly O(V^2 * E).
In unit networks (where capacities are all 1, such as bipartite matching), Dinic runs in O(E * sqrt(V)) time, matching the Hopcroft-Karp bound.

CHAPTER 4: BIPARTITE MATCHING FOR STUDENT-TO-BOOK RESERVATION MATCHING
Consider an academic digital library system where:
- S = {s_1, s_2, ..., s_n} is a set of student reservation requests.
- B = {b_1, b_2, ..., b_m} is a set of available high-demand reserve textbooks.
- Each student s_i has requested a subset of eligible books Preferences(s_i) subseteq B.
The Student Reservation Matching Problem seeks to assign each student at most one book such that the maximum number of students receive a preferred book without exceeding the physical copies available.

Flow Network Reduction:
1. Create a super-source s and super-sink t.
2. Add directed edges from s to each student s_i with capacity 1 (each student can borrow at most 1 book).
3. Add directed edges from student s_i to each book b_j in Preferences(s_i) with capacity 1.
4. Add directed edges from each book b_j to sink t with capacity equal to the number of available copies copies(b_j).
Theorem 4.1 (Integrality Theorem of Network Flow):
If all edge capacities are integers, there exists a maximum flow f where every edge flow f(u, v) is an integer.
Consequently, the maximum flow |f| directly yields an optimal, non-fractional assignment of books to students.
The minimum cut (S, T) identifies the exact set of saturated students and bottleneck book titles where library acquisition budgets should be allocated to eliminate borrowing waitlists.
